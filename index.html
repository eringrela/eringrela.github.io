Erin Grela

Hello, I'm Erin! I'm a fifth-year PhD student in the MIT Economics Department. I work at the intersection of development and environmental economics. My projects focus on how we can more accurately quantify damages from climate change in developing settings, given adaptations that are feasible in these settings, drawing on our understanding of informal economies and taking seriously the structure and timing of adverse weather.

Working Papers

Adaptation to Repeated Weather Shocks

November 2026

Abstract

Climate change is making extreme weather more frequent, which means that households are increasingly likely to experience new shocks before they have fully recovered from prior ones. Whether prior exposure increases resilience through learning and adaptation or reduces it by depleting resources is theoretically ambiguous. I study this question using five waves of LSMS panel data on rural households in Nigeria from 2010 to 2024, linking to daily 250-meter satellite maps of flooding. I estimate how the effects of a flood change when it follows another flood. I organize responses with a model in which a recent flood changes which adaptive measures a household thinks are worth taking, while simultaneously drawing down a household's capacity to finance such adaptations. Substantial adjustments that follow single floods, such as migration, are less likely to recur when that flood is a repeat flood. Households instead shift towards lower upfront-cost coping: they enter subsistence farming, close businesses, and sell assets. These changes are mediated by a household's ex-ante resilience. Among the least resilient households, the costliest actions are taken: the share of children not in school rises by 12.5 percentage points following a repeat flood. At the same time, flood-specific agricultural adaptation rises after a repeat flood among more resilient households. To further test the implications of this framework, I show that a prior hot season produces the same shift toward low upfront-cost coping but no flood-specific adaptation. Finally, I present evidence in line with the cross-sectional literature, showing that households that often experience flooding are less damaged by a given flood. However, even for these households, repeat floods are additionally costly to cope with. Taken together, it seem that the sequence of shocks is important for determining the adaptation response to shocks. In particular, in subsistence settings, prior shocks may narrow the coping mechanisms available to households, shifting coping towards low-cash, in-place responses and, in extreme cases, temporary disinvestment in human capital.
